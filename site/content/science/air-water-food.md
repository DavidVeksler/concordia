## The air

The air in Concordia is Earth's air. The pressure is 101 kPa, the same as sea level, and the gas is oxygen plus an inert diluent, as on Earth. Nitrogen is the natural diluent. NASA's reference sea-level air is 78 percent nitrogen and 21 percent oxygen, and the International Space Station keeps the same pressure and oxygen share. NASA's standard allows crews to live indefinitely anywhere from 34.5 to 103 kPa with at least 30 percent diluent. {% basis "physics" %} Concordia sits near the top of that range. {% basis "design" %}

That air fills about 200 km³ across four rotors. It adds up to some 150 to 200 million tonnes. Oxygen is easy to get, because the Moon's rock is full of it. Nitrogen is scarce on the Moon, and carbonaceous asteroids supply it. (See [how Concordia was built](/station/building-concordia/).)

The air above the artificial sky is the same air as the air below, and the sky is not a pressure boundary. There is no weather except what the sky schedules ([light and sky](/science/light-and-sky/)), and it never rains. Air movement is engineered, and fire protection is built in: low-flammability materials, smoke detection, suppression drones, water mist, mostly electric cooking, and independent refuge zones in large buildings.

Because the air spins with the rotor, it thins slightly toward the axis, the way air thins on a mountain. The ground has the full 101 kPa. At the farms, 1,000 m up, the pressure is about 9 percent lower. {% basis "physics" %}

## Water

Water does more than quench thirst. In Concordia it is shielding, ballast, fire protection and scenery, and it is stored everywhere: in lakes, in structural tanks, in the shielding layers, on the farms, in the reservoirs that balance each rotor, and in the shelters. The inventory is tens of millions of tonnes.

Each rotor holds between 72 and 114 lakes, with the largest about 300 m on a side. Open space takes about a third of the ground in Tsiolkovsky (Rotor 1). The lakes are the visible part of a system that runs continuously: water is collected, cleaned and returned. {% basis "design" %}

The recovery rate matters more than the volume. The first benchmark was set in 2023, when the life support on the International Space Station reached 98 percent water recovery for a crew of a handful. {% basis "physics" %} Concordia runs the same chemistry in city utility plants two decades later, and its target is extremely high recovery rather than an absolutely closed loop. Whatever slips away is replaced with ice from the Moon's poles and from asteroids. {% basis "challenge" %}

## Waste is inventory

There is no landfill. Every kind of waste is a stock of elements, tracked by inventory: water, carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, metals, glass, polymers and biological nutrients. Recycling lines in Ehricke (the industrial rotor) sort and refine what households and factories send back. This is {% term "closed-loop life support" %} on the scale of a city, and the books balance the way a company's do, element by element. {% basis "challenge" %}

A station that recovers 99 percent still loses 1 percent a cycle, so imports never reach zero. The Moon supplies oxygen, silicon, aluminium and iron without strain. The scarce items are carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen, and the {% term "volatiles" %} that carry them come from {% term "carbonaceous asteroid" "carbonaceous asteroids" %}.

## From sewer to supper

The food loop runs as three streams that never mix raw: the sewer, crop residue and the animal tanks. The rule is simple. Nothing from the sewer reaches a plant or an animal until heat has turned it into mineral salts, nitrate and carbon dioxide. {% basis "design" %}

{% diagram "food-loop" "Two lanes: the sewer lane ends in nitrate, salts and CO2 for the crop decks; the food lane turns crops, residue, larvae and air protein into meals." %}

**The sewer.** Toilets flush by vacuum and split urine from solids at the bowl. Urine is under 1 percent of the flow but carries 85 to 90 percent of the nitrogen, 50 to 80 percent of the phosphorus and 80 to 90 percent of the potassium. {% basis "physics" %} Bacteria turn its ammonia into nitrate, the form crops take up best, and it goes to the crop decks as liquid fertiliser. The solids, plate scraps and animal manure go to digesters held at 55 °C, the temperature ESA's MELiSSA life-support loop uses for its first stage, which kills pathogens and yields biogas. The digestate is then burned to a sterile ash. Fire destroys whatever biology survived, along with drug residues and hormones. Acid washes the phosphorus out of the ash, the route Swiss waste law now sets for the country's sewage sludge. {% basis "physics" %} The carbon dioxide goes to the crops and the air-protein vats. Nitrogen that escapes as gas joins the air, which is mostly nitrogen already, so in a sealed city it is stored, not lost.

**Crop residue to larvae.** About half of what a crop grows is not food: stalks, roots, husks and leaves. Some of it grows mushrooms first. The rest, with the trimmings from the process wing, feeds black soldier fly larvae. Dried, the larvae are about 40 percent protein and a third fat, and they turn a fifth to a quarter of their feed's dry matter into body mass. {% basis "physics" %} They are pressed into meal and oil for the fish and the hens. Their frass, the spent bedding and droppings, goes to the parks and gardens. Larvae never eat sewage or plate waste. The European Union bars manure and catering waste as insect feed, and Concordia keeps the same line.

**Fish and hens.** Tilapia grow in recirculating tanks beside the crop decks. Fish water carries ammonia, nitrifying filters turn it into nitrate and the plants drink it, so the fish fertilise the greens. Warm-water fish farms run 1.5 to 2.5 kg of feed per kg of growth, and Concordia's tanks sit at the low end. {% basis "physics" %} Hens lay and broilers grow in barns at 0.6 g in the upper fringe. Fish sludge and manure go back to the digesters.

**Protein from air.** Concordia lives by selling power as computation. Its solar fields draw about 200 GW for the computers that earn its living, eight to ten times the city's own use, at about a cent a kilowatt-hour ([the economy](/station/economy/)). For food, energy is effectively unmetered: every crop deck runs under its own lights, and the city splits water into hydrogen and feeds the hydrogen, with carbon dioxide from the sewer lane and from every breath in the city, to bacteria that need nothing else but minerals. Finland's Solar Foods has made a powder this way since 2024: 78 percent protein, from a 20,000 litre reactor. {% basis "physics" %} Concordia's vats are that reactor at city scale. Their protein goes into food directly, and it fills most of the fish and hen feed, because larvae alone cover only about a quarter of it (see the protein budget below). Precision fermentation in the same halls makes whey and casein for cheese, egg proteins and cooking fats. {% basis "design" %}

## What comes from Earth

About 2 percent of the food by mass is shipped from Earth, roughly 40 g per person a day: what no deck or vat can copy, plus supplements. Wine from named vineyards, whisky aged in Scotland, single-estate coffee and chocolate, aged cheeses and dry-aged beef. Freight to L5 runs about $100 a kilogram, so a bottle of Burgundy arrives carrying $130 of shipping. Across the city that is about 16 tonnes a day and $580 million a year, some $1,500 per resident. {% basis "design" %} For a city of the well-off it is a line item, not a hardship, and every bottle adds a little carbon and nitrogen to the inventory.

## Food in the upper city

Plant food grows at the top of the city, in the upper fringe above about 800 m. Housing stops there. What sits above is an atmospheric service district: crop galleries, nutrient vessels, process halls and utility plants. The air is the shared air of the rotor, and the gravity is 0.6 g at 1,000 m, where rotor 1's farm stands.

Crops grow without soil, in water ({% term "hydroponics" %}) or in a mist around bare roots ({% term "aeroponics" %}), stacked on decks. The farm hall is 288 m long, 124 m wide and 96 m tall, with 12 decks in 24 bays, and four aeroponic towers 72 m tall stand beside it. Next to the crops sit the fish tanks, the hen barns, the larva halls and fermentation vessels that make protein and other food from microbes, and a process wing: wash, sort, pack, bakery, bottling and cold store. A bridge joins the farm to the ascender tower, and ordinary freight carries the food down. {% basis "design" %}

## What it feels like

The smell of basil on a grow deck. A gallery window onto racks of lettuce rising 96 m. At the dispatch dock, crates of salad, trays of loaves and wrapped pallets leave on freight. Food looks like food: tilapia on ice, trays of eggs, wheels of cheese from the fermentation halls. Back at street level, no one thinks about the loop. A tap runs, a lake sits flat under the sky, and a market stall sells salad that was grown a ride up the ascender. At dinner the fish came from the upper fringe and the wine came from France, and the wine cost more to ship than to make. {% basis "fiction" %}

{% deeper "Pressure up and down the rotor" basis="physics" %}
The air co-rotates with the rotor. For air at a constant temperature T with specific gas constant R, pressure rises toward the rim by the same exponential rule as the barometric formula, with the centrifugal potential ω² r² / 2 in place of g × h:

{% eq %}
p(r) = p_axis × exp( ω² r² / (2 R T) )
{% endeq %}

With ω = 0.0626 rad/s, R = 287 J/(kg·K) and T = 293 K, the exponent at the ground is (0.0626 × 2,500)² / (2 × 287 × 293) = 0.146, so the ground pressure is 1.157 times the axis pressure.

| Height | Radius | Pressure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ground | 2,500 m | 101.3 kPa |
| Sky | 2,050 m | 96.6 kPa |
| Crop galleries | 1,500 m | 92.3 kPa |
| Upper frame | 500 m | 88.1 kPa |
| Axis | 0 m | 87.6 kPa |

At the axis the air is as thin as in a town 1,200 m above sea level on Earth. Oxygen keeps its share, so the partial pressure at the axis is 21 percent of 87.6, about 18 kPa. The ground value, 101 kPa, is the one quoted as the habitat pressure.

{% deeper "Why recovery rate dominates" basis="physics" %}
Suppose a water molecule survives each pass through the recycling system with probability R. After n passes the original water remaining is R^n.

| Recovery per pass | After 10 passes | After 50 passes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 93% | 48% | 3% |
| 98% | 82% | 36% |
| 99.9% | 99% | 95% |

Each point of recovery matters. The ISS jump from 93 to 98 percent cut its water losses by about 70 percent. A city of a million people that loses 0.1 percent per pass needs only a small top-up.
{% enddeeper %}

{% deeper "Farm sizing, order of magnitude" basis="design" %}
Rotor 1's grow hall has 12 decks, each 288 m × 124 m:

{% eq %}
area = 12 × 288 × 124 = 4.3 × 10⁵ m²
at 60 kg/m²/year of lettuce-type crop: 2.6 × 10⁴ t/year
{% endeq %}

Spread over a quarter of a million people that is about 100 kg of leafy greens each per year. This is a scale check, not a nutritional plan: calories come from the grain, legume and tuber decks, the vats and the animal loop, and imports are about 2 percent. A fermenter of 150 m³ makes about 2 tonnes of product an hour.
{% enddeeper %}

{% deeper "The protein budget" basis="design" %}
For 400,000 residents eating a rich-country 90 g of protein a day:

{% eq %}
400,000 × 90 g × 365 = 13,100 t of protein a year
{% endeq %}

| Source | Share | Tonnes a year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crops: legumes, grain, nuts, greens | 38% | 5,000 |
| Air protein and fermentation | 25% | 3,300 |
| Fish | 17% | 2,200 |
| Eggs and chicken | 18% | 2,400 |
| Imports | 2% | 260 |

Fish and poultry return about a fifth of their feed protein as edible protein, so 4,600 t on the plate needs about 23,000 t of feed protein. The crops leave about 76,000 t of dry residue and trimmings a year. At 20 percent conversion that grows 15,000 t of dry larvae holding 6,000 t of protein: about a quarter of the feed. The air-protein vats and crop meal supply the other 17,000 t.

Imports at 2 percent of 2 kg of food a day are 40 g per person, 16 t a day for the city and 5,800 t a year. At $100 per kg that is $580 million a year.
{% enddeeper %}
