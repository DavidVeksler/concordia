## Holding in the air

A balloon's skin and a barrel's hoops hold in what pushes outward by pulling along their own length. Engineers call that {% term "hoop stress" %}, and every {% term "pressure vessel" %} carries it.

A rotor's air pulls 257 MN on every metre of its length. The weight of everything inside adds 98 MN and the bands' own weight 28 MN, so the {% term "tension band" "tension bands" %} carry 383 MN per metre. {% basis "physics" %} A {% term "liner" %} of corrugated metal 1.2 mm thick, as in the tanks of LNG ships, keeps the air in. Bundles of carbon rods outside it carry the pull at 550 MPa, about a fifth of their breaking stress. Each bundle is anchored on its own: if one band in ten goes slack, its neighbours rise to 611 MPa. {% basis "challenge" %}

Filled and spinning, a rotor is 8.5 m larger in radius than empty, and every bay line has a {% term "movement joint" %} to take it up. {% basis "design" %}

257 MN | pull per metre of rotor length from the air | physics

## Under the ground

The ground is a floor, neither the air seal nor the main structure. Under its 2 m deck the {% term "rotor wall" %} goes down 28 m more to the liner: a 7 m utility deck of mains; a 10 m transit deck for freight, service vehicles and reservoirs; and an 11 m root deck where the megaframe piers spread out. These three decks are the {% term "underframe" %}. All of it is in the city's air, and crews maintain it in shirtsleeves. {% basis "design" %}

Lawns grow in trays of about 0.3 m of engineered medium. Each tree stands in a {% term "root vessel" %} of about 30 m³, fed from below. Lakes are tanks about 1.5 m deep. Buildings have no foundations: they bolt to sockets in the underframe.

Nothing dropped inside a rotor lands faster than 157 m/s, even from the axis. {% basis "physics" %} The decks and a catch layer stop a rigid 100 t object arriving at 143 m/s, a fall from the top of the megaframe, before it reaches the liner. {% basis "design" %}

## The megaframe and its spokes {#the-megaframe}

A lattice called the {% term "megaframe" %} rises from the ground as far as 1,500 m up. Its ribs, ring beams at 625, 1,000 and 1,500 m, and axial members divide each rotor into 24 cells with bays 654 m around and 480 m long. It thins as weight falls with height. {% basis "design" %}

Piers pass through the ground and spread into roots in the root deck, so a pier's 2 GN reaches the bands as an even 6.4 kPa over its bay instead of a point load that would kink them.

From each pier's crown, at 1,000 or 1,500 m, a {% term "spoke" %} runs on toward the axis: 120 per rotor, carbon bundles about 2.9 m across. They merge in pairs 2,000 m above the ground, and 12 per wheel plane reach a {% term "hub ring" %} that turns with the rotor around the spine.

So the frame has two load paths, outward to the bands and inward to the hub rings. The spokes carry about half the frame's weight, but the bands are sized as if they carried nothing and each spoke for a whole pier's 2 GN, so either path holds alone. {% basis "design" %}

## End walls

Each rotor ends in an inhabited {% term "end wall" "cliff" %} 2,436 m tall, from the ground to the 64 m bore at the axis. Gravity on it falls from 1 g at the foot to 0.6 g at 1,000 m and almost nothing at the top, and 64 percent of its area lies in the first 1,000 m. {% basis "design" %}

The air pushes on one end of one rotor with about 2 trillion newtons, the weight of 200 million tonnes. {% basis "physics" %} A flat plate 2.2 m thick would be stressed to 160 GPa, 294 times what carbon carries; one thick enough would be 38 m of carbon, 1,186 Mt per end. {% basis "physics" %} So the face stays flat for the city, and a separate cap behind it holds the air.

## The end cap {#the-end-cap}

Behind each face, an {% term "end cap" %} holds the air in tension, like the wall's bands: a carbon membrane bulging into the gap in five shallow arcs called {% term "lobe" "lobes" %}, 96, 29, 6, 7 and 13 m deep from the rim inward. Each pulls 100 to 212 MN on every metre at its crown, in carbon 19 to 39 cm thick. {% basis "design" %} Lobes meet on {% term "tie ring" "tie rings" %}, ring girders 850, 1,355, 1,610 and 1,900 m above the ground, with a hub ring 140 m from the axis. The sags balance the sideways pulls on each ring. {% basis "design" %}

{% diagram "end-cap-section" "One cap in section, hub to rim, drawn loaded and four times deeper than wide: the flat face, five lobes with their sags, the tie rings and ties, the rim ring, the cap rooms and the gap with the facing cap." %}

The rings pass that pull to 240 {% term "axial tie" "axial ties" %}, carbon bundles 2,495 m long from one cap to the other above the artificial sky. The rings avoid the megaframe's levels, where the upper city is built: each sits in a corridor no building, pier, spoke or ring beam crosses, with its ties half a pitch off the megaframe's lines. {% basis "design" %} Each tie carries 1.8 to 7.8 GN, where a spoke carries 2 GN. Short struts to the nearest megaframe member hold each at the five rib lines. {% basis "design" %}

{% diagram "end-cap-axial" "The cap seen along the axis, to scale: the rim ring, four tie rings and the hub ring with a dot at every tie anchor, and the radial ribs, with the count, load and section of the ties on each ring." %}

Ties and wall members stretch together, so both caps stay plane and the rotor grows 7.0 m in length under pressure, leaving 102 m clear between facing lobes in the 300 m gap. {% basis "physics" %} If one tie fails, its neighbours carry 1.4 to 1.5 times their load, within the short-term limit. {% basis "challenge" %} Between face and lobes lie the {% term "cap rooms" %}, 0.82 km³ of the city's air per cap. {% basis "design" %}

At the rim, the outer lobe leaves the wall at 25.5 degrees. Its pull has 39.4 MN/m along the axis, taken by the wall's axial members, and 82.8 MN/m inward, which squeezes the corner and which bands cannot take. A rim compression ring of 580 m² of carbon carries it, 174 GN. {% basis "challenge" %}

The two caps of a rotor weigh 85.6 Mt. With them a rotor's mass comes to about 341 Mt, which is the figure the station now plans for each rotor. {% basis "design" %}

## The gap and the stationary shell {#the-stationary-shell}

Outside the rotor wall is a vacuum {% term "gap" %}. Air there would drag on the spinning wall: 100 to 140 GW of friction per rotor, against a station that runs on 40 GW. {% basis "physics" %} The gap only has to be wide enough for the wall's growth, tolerances and maintenance crawlers, so the stationary shell's inner face sits 20 m outside the skin. {% basis "design" %}

Light crosses the gap at any width, so the outer skin is the rotor's main radiator. Panels on it shine the rotor's 7.1 GW of heat at a cooled absorber on the shell's inner face. [Power and heat](/science/power-and-heat/) follows it out. {% basis "design" %}

The shell is a stationary open truss 21 km long carrying shielding cassettes, radiators and docks. It holds no pressure: a punctured panel loses mass and nothing else. {% basis "design" %}

## What it feels like

You never see the bands. You can see the underframe through a glass floor at a pier's plinth or on a maintenance tour. Above the artificial sky, from the upper plates or a glider, the spokes are fine lit lines running up to the axis. {% basis "fiction" %}

Each rotor was brought to speed in small steps, pausing to look for resonances. As it filled and turned, the joint on every bay line opened 2.2 m. {% basis "physics" %} [Building Concordia](/station/building-concordia/) tells that story.

{% deeper "Loads on the rotor wall" basis="physics" %}
A cylinder of radius r at pressure P pulls on its wall with P × r per metre of length. The air presses on the liner, 30 m below the ground:

{% eq %}
N_air = 101,682 Pa × 2,530 m = 257 MN/m
N = 257 + 98 (weight) + 28 (bands) = 383 MN/m
thickness = N / working stress
{% endeq %}

| Band | Stress | Thickness | Per rotor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon rods | 550 MPa | 0.70 m | 44 Mt |
| Steel wire | 600 MPa | 0.88 m | 275 Mt |

Steel's own weight raises its load to 528.5 MN/m, and four rotors of it would be 1,100 Mt, over half the station. Carbon is 176 Mt.
{% enddeeper %}

{% deeper "Why the wall stretches" basis="physics" %}
At 550 MPa the carbon rods stretch 0.335 percent, and the ring with them:

{% eq %}
0.00335 × 2,530 m = 8.5 m of radius
2π × 8.5 m = 53 m of circumference = 2.2 m per bay
{% endeq %}

Two thirds comes with the air, a third with the spin.

{% diagram "wall-stretch" "Three bays empty and loaded. The stretch is drawn about 40 times larger than true; the labels carry the true values." %}
{% enddeeper %}

{% deeper "Why the megaframe thins with height" basis="physics" %}
{% eq %}
g(h) = (2,500 − h) / 2,500 × 1 g
cantilever limit: L_max = 40 m / g
{% endeq %}

A plate reaches 40 m from a pier at 1 g and 100 m at 0.4 g, so piers shrink from 45 m square at the base to 15 m at 1,500 m.
{% enddeeper %}

{% deeper "The force on an end wall" basis="physics" %}
{% eq %}
F = P × π × r² = 101,325 × π × 2,500² = 1.99 × 10¹² N
{% endeq %}
{% enddeeper %}

{% deeper "How the end cap holds the air" basis="physics" %}
For an arc of radius R with its crown at radius r_c, the pull is largest at the inner edge, and half as much around it:

{% eq %}
N(r) = P × R × (r_c + r) / (2r)
N_hoop = P × R / 2
lobe 1: N_crown = 101,325 Pa × 988.8 m = 100.2 MN/m
thickness = 100.2 MN/m / 550 MPa = 18.7 cm
F = P × π × (2,500² − 64²) = 1,988 GN, shared by the rings
{% endeq %}

{% enddeeper %}

{% deeper "The corner in numbers" basis="physics" %}
A rounded knuckle needs 351 m of depth along the axis; keeping 100 m clear between facing caps leaves 1 m per cap. Letting the wall bend moves its end 49 m inward and strains the bands 1.9 percent. So the ring takes 69.6 of the 82.8 MN/m at 300 MPa. It is built 12.8 m oversize: the wall grows 8.2 m under load and the ring shortens 4.6 m.
{% enddeeper %}

{% deeper "Why a vacuum" basis="physics" %}
Air at one atmosphere over a rotor's wall would cost 143 GW at a friction coefficient of 0.0015; one percent of an atmosphere, 1.4 GW. Across vacuum, heat moves only as infrared:

{% eq %}
q = σ (T₁⁴ − T₂⁴) / (1/ε₁ + 1/ε₂ − 1)
{% endeq %}

At emissivity 0.9, 350 K panels facing a 320 K absorber move 210 W/m², so 7.1 GW needs 85 percent of the skin; 360 K panels against 300 K need 44 percent.
{% enddeeper %}
