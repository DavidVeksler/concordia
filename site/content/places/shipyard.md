## What you see

The yard sits at the forward end of the station, between Ehricke (the industrial rotor) and the spaceport. A frame of eight huge rim rings, each a 16-sided lattice 5 km across and 100 m deep, joined by eight longerons, encloses the work. The shell here is an open cage, so the yard is visible from outside and the Sun shines in.

At its centre floats **Meridian 04**, the fourth hull of a long-haul crew and cargo class. She is 700 m long and 76 m across her tanks, and she is unfinished. From the bow back, her skin is white and gold. Then comes a crew drum with its shielding plates about 60 percent on, then a drum cut open to show 25 stacked decks, then a bare frame with cabin pods waiting beside it. Eight hydrogen tanks sit on a keel truss, some wrapped in foil and some still empty cradles. At the stern are the reactor behind its shadow shield, four radiator wings and four engine mounts, two of them fitted.

## How a ship is held

Nothing carries her weight. Three open lattice rings, each with eight padded capture arms, touch the hull only at structural frames. Each ring hangs from the yard ribs on 8 to 12 pre-tensioned lines, 1.8 m thick and 2 to 2.5 km long, spread like a web. Nine small tugs fire short pulses to hold her still when a delivered tank shifts the centre of mass.

Tanks, drums, truss lengths and cabin pods arrive from the industrial rotor's logistics end on cargo movers, then cross a straight lane to the ship at 20 to 40 m/s, braking over the last 200 m.

About 40 drones carry fasteners and inspection cameras. Eight heavy manipulators, reaching 80 to 150 m, place the modules. Crawlers weld and sinter seams. Six pressurized capsules hold people near the open section, so a person can look at the work in person without an EVA.

## Why zero g

Gravity makes big structures expensive. A very large tank, a truss kilometres long or a telescope mirror needs no extra strength to hold itself up in the yard, only to survive its working loads. The yard is reserved for jobs that gain from weightlessness or hard vacuum: spacecraft assembly, very large tanks, telescope assembly, truss fabrication, vacuum metallurgy and oversized additive manufacturing. Finished ships leave radially through the open cage.

{% shot "sc-exterior-yard-through-cage" "Meridian 04 seen from outside through the open cage: hanging in the middle of the yard with no floor and no cradle under her. Nothing carries her weight." %}

The material comes from the industrial rotor next door. See [The industrial rotor](/places/industrial-rotor/) for the stores and [Materials](/science/materials/) for the lunar and asteroid sources. For what a ship like this does once she leaves, see [Getting there](/science/getting-there/): she is nuclear-thermal, with a {% term "specific impulse" %} of about 900 seconds.

{% deeper "Meridian 04 in detail" %}
Meridian 04 holds about 226,000 m³ of liquid hydrogen in eight tanks, each 20 m across and 90 m long, and masses 25 to 30 kt dry. The reactor sits about 300 m behind the crew drums, so distance plus a shadow shield does most of the radiation shielding. That sets the class length of 500 to 800 m: a shorter hull cannot hold this much hydrogen with the reactor that far back.

At 70.8 kg/m³ the hydrogen is about 16,000 t. With an exhaust speed of 900 × 9.81 = 8.8 km/s and a 27,500 t dry ship, the rocket equation gives Δv = 8.8 × ln(43,500 ÷ 27,500) = about 4 km/s on full tanks.
