## A passenger’s next stop

The whole port is one straight line on the station axis. The express from the city ends at a terminus drum. Beyond it come a zero-g hall, two counter-rotating lounge wheels on one axle, a second zero-g hall of gates, a small-craft apron and the passenger pier. The picture is the classic one: two wheels on an axle, a flower of docked ships in front, the city cylinder behind.

{% shot "pl-spaceport-hero" "The spaceport from outside: a lounge wheel on its axle with the gate hall and its berth frames in front of it, and the P1 trunk running off to the right." %}

## What you see

Arrival starts in the axis hall, 200 m long and in zero g, in the same Art Deco language as the city: stone ribs, warm light coves and bronze-framed windows. A monumental arch stands under a lit panel reading WELCOME TO CONCORDIA. Nobody is ever more than 15 m from something to hold: lines of grab handles carry travellers along the hall at walking pace, a cable runs down the axis, and a catch net spans the hall every 60 m. The far end opens onto the spindle and the first lounge wheel.

{% shot "pl-spaceport-terminal" "The axis hall looking forward: ribbed walls, shops and benches on the deck, handline riders overhead and the round opening of the spindle at the far end." %}

A narrow stationary spindle runs through the middle of the two wheels. It carries three sealed passages, so arriving and departing streams never meet: green for the landside, amber for departures bound for the gates, blue for arrivals coming in from them. This is the airport split of a sterile side and a public side, built into the axle.

### Two wheels, one gravity ladder

Both wheels turn at 2 rpm, one way and then the other, so most of their {% term "angular momentum" %} cancels. At 2 rpm gravity depends only on radius, and the wheels are built to give three useful values: **0.166 g** at the hub ring, the lunar value, 37 m from the axis; **0.38 g** at the Mars landing, 85 m out; and **1.00 g** on the deck, 223.6 m out.

You enter by a **carousel**. A chamber at the stationary end is boarded at rest and spun up over 60 seconds, so by the time the door opens the wheel's motion is yours. Then a gondola lift runs out along a spoke from the lunar ring to the Mars landing to the deck, tilting a few degrees as it climbs so the floor stays under you. The ride takes 49 seconds without stops.

{% shot "sc-spaceport-luna-ring" "The hub ring at 0.166 g: a floor that curves up on both sides, a stair and a few travellers." %}

### The street that curves up

The deck is a street 1.4 km round, inside a tube 24 m high. Because you are inside a wheel, the street does not run away from you. It rises. Fifty metres ahead the floor is 5.6 m above your feet, and a hundred metres ahead it is 22 m up, so the street climbs into the vault in front of you and the people walking it seem to hang above. Shops, restaurants, a family area, security and the boarding hall line the deck. Sleeping cabins sit below it in the keel.

{% shot "sc-spaceport-rim-street" "Wheel D's deck looking spinward: the street rising into the vault, with travellers walking in 1 g." %}

Drop a coin and you can see the wheel spin. Released 1.5 m above the floor, it lands 11.7 cm antispinward of the point under your hand on the deck, 19.1 cm at the Mars landing and 29.7 cm on the lunar ring, because you are being carried round while it falls.

### The Star Floor

A few glazed bays in the floor of the departures wheel look straight down into space. The view makes a full turn every 30 seconds, stars and the station's trusswork wheeling past under your feet, and when a bay faces the sun, light floods up through the glass.

{% shot "sc-spaceport-star-floor" "Looking down through a glazed Star Floor bay in the deck of wheel D: bronze mullions over a drop into space." %}

### The gap

The two wheels turn against each other 50 m apart, at a relative 96 m/s at their rims, so nothing ever crosses between them. A stationary catwalk in the gap gives the odd view of the whole port: two hub drums face each other, with chevrons on their end plates pointing the opposite ways they turn.

{% shot "sc-spaceport-wheel-gap" "The 50 m gap between the wheels from a catwalk: the hub drums face each other, their end plates marked with chevrons." %}

### Gates and coaches

After the lounge you ride the lift up, cross to the amber passage by a second carousel and float into the gate hall. Four coach berths, A01 to A04, sit at the points of a cross, each with a telescoping boarding bridge and a berth frame. A coach docks nose-in at its berth: it comes in through the open port, stops over the berth and slides radially in for the last 263 m. Four small ports, S1 to S4, take couriers. Crews reach cargo and tug bays by their own route, so freight never shares a bridge with passengers. Passengers for Earth board a coach here, and a tug tows the loaded stack round the port to the staging guideway on the hull, where it rides to the launcher. Flights to the Moon leave the gate under their own thrusters.

{% shot "sc-spaceport-gate" "Gate A03: the boarding board and plate beside the arch, and the neck to the docked coach." %}

### Piers and the propellant farm

Large ships do not dock inside. They berth at three piers on the outer face of the port, each a deep truss running 900 m. Articulated arms clamp a ship alongside, and freighters are 260 m long. Pier P1 is the passenger pier, with a pressurized trunk that runs straight on from the axis.

{% shot "pl-spaceport-pier" "A freighter alongside a truss pier on the outer face of the port, with tugs and the axial aperture." %}

{% shot "sci-shipyard-close" "Nearby, the zero-g shipyard: Meridian 04 held in her capture rings, with tension lines spread to the yard frame." %}

Beyond the piers, outside the station body, is the propellant farm: two liquid hydrogen spheres, two liquid oxygen spheres and two storable-propellant cylinders under sunshades, with cryocoolers that re-liquefy the boil-off. It sits clear of the approach lane and well away from the city's pressure vessels, so a tank failure cannot reach a home.

{% shot "pl-propellant-farm" "Foil spheres and storable tanks of the propellant farm under sunshades, with radiators and transfer lines." %}

## How it works in brief

The port is stationary and weightless where ships dock, and rotating only where people wait. That keeps docking simple, because craft meet a fixed frame, and keeps waiting comfortable, because gravity sits in two small wheels instead of the whole terminal. The wheels sit in full sunlight, clear of the shadow of the sun skirt. The express that brings you here is described in [The axis and the spine](/places/axis-and-spine/), and the ride that follows a coach to Earth is in [Getting there](/science/getting-there/).

{% deeper "Wheels, carousel and docking" %}
The deck circles 1,405 m, with six spokes, inside a tube whose vault crown is 24 m over the floor. Walking spinward at 1.4 m/s you weigh about 1.06 g, and walking the other way 0.94 g. A wheel with a hull-grade shield weighs about 634 kt and holds 0.95 million m3 of air.

The carousel has eight chambers of 24 people. A 60 s ramp keeps the sideways push to 0.0078 g, and the eight chambers move about 2,300 people an hour each way. The lift takes 88 s with a stop at the Mars landing and tilts at most 25 degrees.

The port runs along the axis from the terminus drum at x 9,400 m to the end of the passenger trunk at x 10,490 m: axis hall 9,560 to 9,760, wheel rims 9,870 to 9,980, gate hall 10,040 to 10,240. A tow from gate A01 to the staging guideway at x 9,728 m covers 4.7 km at 10 m/s. A coach approaches along a corridor 400 m from the axis, then closes radially to the berth frame mouth at 137 m. A berth collar sits 52 m from the axis.

The propellant farm begins about 11,420 m along the station, at least 355 m outside the 1,500 m approach cylinder and about 1.4 km beyond the nearest defence battery. The farm holds spheres of 16 m radius (hydrogen) and 12 m radius (oxygen), and cylinders of 6 m radius.
