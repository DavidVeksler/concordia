## Why you cannot launch a city

The station's finished mass is about 1.92 x 10^12 kg, two billion tonnes: four rotors at 300 million tonnes each, Ehricke (the industrial rotor) at 100 million, and about 621 million tonnes of shielding. The International Space Station weighs 419,725 kg. Concordia is about 4.6 million of them. {% basis "design" %}

Even with lower launch costs, transporting the station's full mass would be prohibitively expensive. In 2018 NASA's Harry Jones put the Space Shuttle at $54,500 per kg to low Earth orbit and Falcon 9 at about $2,720. {% basis "physics" %} By the 2030s fully reusable heavy launchers carry more than 100 tonnes a flight for about $2 million, refuelled in orbit by tankers: about $20 per kg to low orbit and about $100 per kg delivered to L5. At that price two billion tonnes is 20 million full loads and about $200 trillion. {% basis "fiction" %}

Launch therefore supplied the initial equipment and crews. The haulers, the first mining machines, the seed factory and the construction crews all flew up from Earth, and Earth still supplies electronics, medicines and specialist parts. Bulk construction uses material already in space. {% explore viewpoint="exterior-25km-silhouette" label="See the 21 km silhouette" %}

## Step 1: feedstock flows, mid 2030s

{% diagram "construction-sequence" "Five steps, factory first." %}

Two streams of raw material start arriving at L5 in the mid 2030s.

{% shot "sci-ov-material-flow" "The material-flow overlay: lunar feedstock and asteroid volatiles arrive at the stores in Ehricke, then flow on to the city, the yard and the hull." %}

**From asteroids, first.** Small metal-rich near-Earth asteroids, tens of metres across, are bagged whole and hauled into the L5 region without a landing. Their iron-nickel goes directly to the yard without smelting. The Moon lacks carbon, nitrogen and hydrogen, so small carbonaceous asteroids follow the same route. Measurements in 2045 put the captured carbonaceous bodies at roughly 10 percent water and a few percent carbon by mass. {% basis "fiction" %} How the haulers work is on [Mining the Moon and asteroids](/science/mining/).

**From the Moon, at scale.** Lunar mining and refining, and an electromagnetic {% term "mass driver" %}, come up behind the asteroid haulers and soon carry most of the tonnage. The Moon has 1/6 of Earth's surface gravity (1.62 m/s2 against 9.80) and an escape velocity of 2.38 km/s against Earth's 11.2 km/s. It supplies most of the oxygen, silicon, aluminium, iron, glass and shielding mass.

{% callout "In 2045" %}
Prospecting in the 2030s found water ice at a few percent by mass through the upper metres of {% term "permanently shadowed region" "permanently shadowed" %} regolith, across several hundred square kilometres of the lunar poles. It is enough for propellant and early life support. It is not enough for the air and water of a million people, which is why asteroids supply most of the {% term "volatiles" %}. {% basis "fiction" %}
{% endcallout %}

## Step 2: the station is its own factory

Construction began with the zero-g yard, power supply, radiators and first industrial plant. Those facilities form Ehricke (the industrial rotor) and the shipyard. The seed factory first produced more manufacturing equipment: AI-designed machines built the next machines, doubling output every few months ([who paid for it, and why](/station/economy/)).

{% shot "sc-zero-g-shipyard" "The zero-g yard, one of the first parts built. The factory comes before the homes: it makes the girders, tanks and cassettes the city is made from." %}

Workers live in small rotating construction rings, a few hundred metres across. The factory turns incoming feedstock into girders, glass, fibre, tanks and cassettes, so that later steps draw on stock held in the station, not on Earth. The industrial rotor still holds stores from both sources: sintered regolith billets, aluminium and iron ingots, glass and basalt fibre and oxygen from the Moon; water ice, carbon feedstock, nitrogen compounds and metal concentrates from asteroids.

## Step 3: the superstructure and the shield

The 21 km stationary truss goes up around the yard. This deep spatial truss supports the station's external systems without containing its atmosphere. Shielding cassettes of compacted regolith and slag are fixed to its outside. One metre of regolith at 1.6 t/m3 over the 388 km2 of hull is roughly 621 million tonnes, supplied from the Moon.

{% shot "sci-ext-1km-patchwork" "The truss is the frame and the cassettes are the shield. From 1 km the hull reads as a patchwork of pale tiles, each one replaceable." %}

## Step 4: Tsiolkovsky (Rotor 1) and the first spin-up

Tsiolkovsky is built stationary. The prebuilt megaframe goes in while the rotor is still, with only construction gravity. The rotor is pressure-tested, then given atmosphere, then spun up.

{% shot "st-megaframe-base" "The megaframe was built while the rotor was still, then loaded for full spin." %}

Spin-up is slow on purpose. The first full-speed turn of a structure this large is one of the riskiest events in construction. Speed rises in small increments and dwells at each step to expose resonances. The next increment starts only when the structure behaves as the model says. Temporary construction equipment comes out before full gravity. The campaign takes days to weeks. {% basis "fiction" %}

The air for one rotor is about 45 million tonnes. Most of its nitrogen comes from asteroids. The first residents arrive in 2041.

## Step 5: Noordung, Bernal and O'Neill

Rotors fill one at a time, so a defect found in rotor 1 can be corrected before the others take people. Rotors 2, 3 and 4 (Noordung, Bernal and O'Neill) follow. The fourth rotor reaches full spin in 2044. The construction rings are not taken apart: they are towed 200 km out to the next build site, where the yards have started the next station ([who pays](/station/economy/)).

## What the build looked like

You can still see the stage Concordia went through. The next station is going up 200 km away in the same order: an open spine, one bare rotor closed, hoop frames for the second, about a third of the shield placed, and the old construction rings turning at the yard. {% basis "fiction" %} {% explore viewpoint="next-station-yard" label="See it in the sim" %}

{% deeper "The numbers behind the build" %}
- Finished station: about 1.92 x 10^12 kg. Four rotors at 3 x 10^11 kg each (about 45 Mt of air included in each) make 1.2 x 10^12 kg, the industrial rotor 1 x 10^11 kg, and the shielding example 6.2 x 10^11 kg. The full table is on [Concordia by the numbers](/station/by-the-numbers/).
- Atmosphere for all four rotors: 150 to 200 million tonnes, in almost 200 km3.
- Shield example: 1 m of regolith at 1.6 t/m3 over 388 km2, about 621 million tonnes.
- Launch price: about $2 million a flight for 100 t is $20/kg to low orbit; four tanker flights per load bound for L5 make it about $100/kg there. 1.92 x 10^12 kg / 100 t = 1.9 x 10^7 loads; x $100/kg = $1.9 x 10^14, about $200 trillion. At the 2018 Falcon 9 price it would have been $5.2 x 10^15.
- Escape energy per kg: Earth 0.5 x (11.2 km/s)2 = 62.7 MJ; Moon 0.5 x (2.38 km/s)2 = 2.8 MJ, about 22 times less.
- Partial gravity: long-term data on living at 0.2 to 0.6 g is still being gathered. {% basis "challenge" %} Children, pregnancy and routine healthcare stay near 1 g in the rotor cities. {% basis "design" %}
{% enddeeper %}
